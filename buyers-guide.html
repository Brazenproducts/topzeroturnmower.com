Zero Turn Mower Buyer's Guide

Everything you need to know before you spend $2,500–5,000 on a zero turn mower. Deck sizes, engine types, terrain considerations, and what to inspect on a used machine.

Step 1: Know Your Acreage

Start here. This drives every other decision. Zero turns scale based on deck width, and deck width should match your lot size and obstacle density.

Step 2: Assess Your Terrain

Flat, open lawn and sloped, obstacle-dense property are very different use cases. If you have slopes above 10 degrees, look specifically for machines with wider stances (Ariens IKON XD) or explicit slope ratings in their documentation. If you have lots of trees and beds, prioritize a smaller deck with a tight turning radius over raw width.

Wet ground is a separate concern. Heavy zero turns (600+ lbs) will rut soft ground if you mow when it's wet. Lightweight models fare better but still shouldn't be run on saturated soil. Schedule mowing for after the ground has had 24–48 hours to firm up after rain.

Step 3: Fabricated vs. Stamped Deck

This matters more than most buyers realize. A fabricated deck is welded from thick-gauge steel plate — significantly stronger and more durable. A stamped deck is pressed from a single sheet of lighter-gauge metal. Both cut fine when new. After a rock strike or years of regular use, the fabricated deck holds its shape and resists cracking. The stamped deck may develop stress cracks or warp.

Rule of thumb: anything above $2,500 should have a fabricated deck. If a machine at that price point has a stamped deck, that's a red flag about the manufacturer's cost priorities.

Step 4: Engine Brand Matters

The engine is the most expensive component to replace and the single biggest predictor of long-term reliability. Here's the hierarchy:

  • Kawasaki FR/FX series — Industry gold standard. 2,000+ hour lifespan well documented. Parts available everywhere.
  • Kohler 7000 series — Extremely reliable, excellent parts availability, easy to service. Slightly behind Kawasaki in long-term data but close.
  • Briggs & Stratton Professional series — Solid mid-tier. Service infrastructure solid.
  • OEM branded engines (Toro, Cub Cadet, etc.) — Manufactured by Briggs or Kohler typically. Fine, but you're locked into the brand ecosystem for service.

Step 5: Buying Used

A used zero turn can be a great value — these machines are overbuilt and last a long time. What to inspect:

  • Hour meter: Under 300 hours on a maintained machine is ideal. 300–600 is fine. Over 800 on a residential machine warrants a deep mechanical inspection.
  • Deck underside: Look for cracks in the deck shell, bent spindle housings, and scoring on the underside from hitting hard objects.
  • Engine cold start: Listen for ticking or knocking after a cold start. Normal engines are quiet after 30 seconds of warmup.
  • Hydrostatic drive: Test both lap bars independently. Motion should be smooth and symmetrical — if one side is sluggish, the pump may be failing.
  • Belt condition: Fraying, glazing, or cracking means immediate replacement. Budget $50–150 for a belt set.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I look for when buying a used zero turn mower?

Check the hour meter first — anything under 300 hours with good maintenance history is a solid buy. Inspect the deck underside for cracks, bent spindles, and rust through. Start the engine cold and listen for unusual ticking or knocking. Test both drive controls independently.

What maintenance does a zero turn mower need?

Oil change every 50 hours. Air filter every 25 hours. Spark plugs every 100 hours. Deck belt inspection quarterly. Blade sharpening every 20–25 hours. Grease all fittings at season start. Clean under the deck after every mow.

How do I know what size zero turn mower I need?

Under 1 acre: 42-inch deck. 1–2 acres: 42–54 inch deck depending on obstacle density. 2–3 acres: 54-inch deck. 3–5 acres: 54–60 inch deck. Engine size should follow deck size.

Should I buy a zero turn at a dealer or a big box store?

For machines above $2,500, a dealer is strongly preferred. Dealers do the pre-delivery inspection, set deck pitch correctly, and are your warranty service point. The price difference is usually small; the service difference is significant.

What's a fabricated deck vs a stamped deck?

A fabricated deck is welded from thick steel plate — stiffer, heavier, and more impact-resistant. A stamped deck is pressed from thinner steel — lighter and cheaper. Fabricated decks handle rock strikes and rough conditions better. Look for fabricated decks on anything above $2,500.
